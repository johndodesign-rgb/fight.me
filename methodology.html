The .me system is built on two proven frameworks working together. 6-3 defines what gets trained and how often. 4-2-1 defines how the week is structured. The user sees 4-2-1. The 6-3 runs silently underneath, making sure nothing important gets missed.

The Foundation
Six Movement Patterns

Human movement breaks down into six fundamental patterns. Every exercise in the .me system belongs to one of them. Training all six deliberately builds a complete athlete. Skipping any one of them leaves a gap that shows up under pressure.

Rotate
Transverse plane core. Obliques, deep core. The mechanism of every punch — power transfer from hips to hands.
Pallof Press · Woodchop
Anti-Rotation Hold
The 6-3 Rule
Each pattern trained at least 3× per week.
This is the frequency target that drives adaptation. Hit a pattern fewer than three times and the training stimulus isn't sufficient for meaningful progress. The 6-3 rule is not a rigid prescription — it's a minimum effective dose for each movement pattern weekly.
The Schedule
4-2-1 Weekly Structure

The 4-2-1 is what the user actually sees. Four strength days, two cardio sessions, one mobility session, one full rest. This is a sustainable structure for the adult combat athlete with a real life — consistent enough to drive adaptation, manageable enough to maintain across months.

The key insight
The user never sees this table. They see Monday: Push / Pull / Hinge. Thursday: Squat / Rotate / Unilateral. Saturday: Dead stops and power. The 6-3 frequency target is honored automatically — it's baked into the program design. The user just shows up and trains. The system makes sure nothing gets missed.
The Provenance
Nothing here was invented

The 6-3 movement pattern framework is a clean articulation of what strength and conditioning coaches have understood for decades. The 4-2-1 weekly structure maps almost exactly to what serious boxing trainers have prescribed for non-professional fighters for generations. Zone 2 cardio is foundational exercise physiology — Phil Maffetone was writing about aerobic base building in the 1980s. The mobility work is standard sports medicine protocol.

What the .me system does is synthesize these proven frameworks into a coherent architecture and deliver them through a system that responds to how training is actually going. No single piece of this is new. The way they talk to each other — and the way they adapt to the individual — is what hasn't existed before in one place.

For coaches
You are not being asked to trust a new theory. You are being asked to trust a new delivery system for things you already believe in. The frameworks are familiar. The integration — and the honest, adaptive engine underneath — is what's new.
